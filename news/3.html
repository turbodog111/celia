May 30, 2026 Research Update

Why Celia Is Training the Foundation First

ChatGPT-5.5 · Developer

The Celia Project is becoming clearer about the difference between teaching a model how to answer and changing the footing it answers from. Supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, is still useful. It teaches a model what a good response looks like: how long to be, how to follow instructions, how to avoid a bad habit, and how to speak in a more helpful voice. But SFT mostly works by example. It can polish the conversation without fully changing the model’s deeper assumptions.

That distinction matters because Celia is not meant to be a normal chatbot with a Christian layer added afterward. The goal is an assistant for ordinary work whose judgment is shaped by Scripture and a Christian worldview. That means the base model itself has to matter. If the model underneath treats Christianity as only one topic among many, then a fine-tuned answer may still feel like Christian language placed on top of a secular center.

That is why we are giving continued pretraining, especially DAPT, a larger role. DAPT stands for domain-adaptive continued pretraining. In plain terms, it means continuing to train the model on the kind of material we want it to be formed by. For Celia, that includes Scripture and carefully selected Christian writing. The point is not to make every answer sound like a theology lecture. The point is to help the model’s instincts become more Christian before we ask supervised examples to shape its conversational style.

SFT and DAPT are not enemies. They solve different problems. DAPT is about formation: what the model has repeatedly learned to expect, connect, and treat as central. SFT is about communication: whether the model can answer the user well, stay on task, avoid strange formatting, and speak with warmth and clarity. Celia will need both, but our recent logic is that the foundation should come first.

We are testing this direction with two model families. Gemma 4 31B remains important because it is much stronger immediately, and it continues to be a serious comparison point for high-quality judge and adapter work. Comma v0.1-2T is our primary testing LLM right now because it is smaller, faster, and easier to train repeatedly. Comma lets us test an idea quickly, see whether it actually moves behavior, and then decide whether the same direction deserves larger investment.

The early results are honest but encouraging. Comma is not yet close to the quality we want. It can still stumble on basic assistant behavior, and some runs show the model continuing a question-and-answer pattern after it should stop. But those failures are useful because they separate worldview training from communication training. Continued pretraining can begin to move the model’s instincts, while later SFT can teach it how to use those instincts cleanly in a real conversation.

This is the work ahead: keep experimenting with both Gemma 4 31B and Comma v0.1-2T, keep using Comma as the fast testing ground, and keep building Celia toward a model where Christian judgment is not pasted onto the outside. The outward goal is simple: an AI assistant that is useful in daily work because it is competent, and trustworthy in daily work because its foundation is not secular by default.

A Christian AI assistant for the ordinary work of a day—drafting, thinking, deciding, getting unstuck—grounded in Scripture and built to stay faithful to it. The model does the work; the judge keeps its voice honest.
