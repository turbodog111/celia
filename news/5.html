The Celia Project: May 2026 Report

The Celia Project Team

May was the month The Celia Project moved from a promising local experiment into a clearer research program. The project entered the month focused on improving judge adapters and left it with two defined model lines, a public website, a stronger curation process, and a more serious long-term direction: build a capable Christian AI assistant whose foundation is shaped by Scripture rather than merely decorated with Christian language afterward.

The Gemma Series became the project’s quality gate. During May, the judge line advanced through V1.5.0, V1.5.0-r2, and V1.5.0-r2-combined. V1.5.0 marked the start of the modern judge series. V1.5.0-r2 expanded the same direction with stronger curated material. V1.5.0-r2-combined then became the best judge the project has built so far. These releases helped reveal which kinds of candidate answers Celia should reject: answers that were useful but impersonal, answers that sounded warm but vague, answers that gave Christian advice in a checklist form, and answers that were morally clear but still lacked Celia’s voice. By the time V1.5.0-r2-combined was produced, the Gemma Series had become more than a model checkpoint. It had become the practical quality gate for deciding what belongs in Celia’s future training.

The project also made an important naming and architecture reset. The old history had become crowded with patch names, partial iterations, and legacy references that no longer reflected the actual direction of the work. May brought a cleanup of that structure. The strongest judge releases were preserved, older Gemma material was archived rather than treated as the active center, and the project was reorganized around the two series that now matter: the Gemma Series and the Comma Alpha Series. That change was not cosmetic. It made it easier to see what each model is for, what has actually been tested, and what should be trained next.

The Comma Alpha Series became the path toward ownership. The most important strategic decision in May was the move from treating Gemma 4 31B as the center of Celia toward using Comma v0.1-2T as the main owned-model testing ground. Gemma remains valuable and powerful, especially as a judge and reference point. But the long-term goal of The Celia Project is not simply to attach an adapter to a strong outside model. The long-term goal is to build a model that can be shaped, trained, licensed, and improved as Celia. Comma gives the project a smaller, faster foundation where experiments can happen repeatedly and where the effects of continued training can be studied more directly.

The Alpha work clarified what has to be solved next. May’s Comma experiments produced the first Celia Alpha base releases, including A0.1, A0.1.1, and A0.1.2, along with early supervised training trials on top of that base. Those runs showed that changing a model’s foundation and teaching it to communicate well are related but not identical problems. Continued pretraining can begin moving the model’s instincts toward Christian material, while supervised instruction work teaches the model how to answer in a clean, helpful, human-readable way. Early Comma results show clear room to grow, and that is exactly their value: they mark out the real tasks ahead—stronger single-answer formatting, better ordinary reasoning, more natural Christian application, and less generic secular framing in everyday prompts. The next prepared Alpha work builds directly on that lesson.

The website turned the work outward. May also saw the creation and rapid improvement of The Celia Project’s public website. What began as a prototype became a real front door for the project: a landing page, mission page, Judge Models section, news section, contact page, and visual identity built around a blue research-lab style with a warmer Christian mission underneath. The Judge Models page now explains the difference between the Gemma Series and the Comma Alpha Series in plain language, and the News page gives visitors a running view of the project’s direction.

The guiding idea has become simpler and stronger: Celia should be useful for ordinary work because she is competent, and trustworthy in ordinary work because her judgment is not secular by default. May did not finish that mission. It made the mission clearer. The project now has a proven judge line, a serious owned-model path, a cleaner internal structure, and a public presentation that can grow with the research. June begins with that foundation in place.

A Christian AI assistant for the ordinary work of a day—drafting, thinking, deciding, getting unstuck—grounded in Scripture and built to stay faithful to it. The model does the work; the judge keeps its voice honest.
